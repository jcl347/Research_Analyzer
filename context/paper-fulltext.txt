Long-Run Growth
A Thermo-Ecological Augmentation of the Solow Model

Independent Researcher

Abstract
This paper develops a thermo-ecological augmentation of the Solow growth model
that treats production as a physical throughput process generating both useful
output and unavoidable thermodynamic waste. We introduce three coupled state
variables absent from the standard framework: (i) physical thermodynamic
output (TO), the energy waste, emissions, and material inefficiency created as a
byproduct of production, valued at shadow prices derived from the social cost of
carbon and analogous pollutant valuations; (ii) health capital (H), a stock
variable linking pollution exposure to effective labor supply, cognitive performance,
and total factor productivity; and (iii) natural capital (R), resource stocks subject
to depletion and regeneration dynamics.
We derive the modified Solow steady state in closed form and show that it depends
on a thermo-ecological multiplier Ω = ϕ(TO/Y) · ζ(H) · θ(H) that is strictly less
than unity when thermodynamic waste and health damage are positive. The steady-
state capital intensity and output per worker are reduced by a factor that depends
on pollution intensity, health capital, and the elasticity of damage. We characterize
the thermodynamic growth trap: economies that allow waste intensity to rise
converge to permanently lower balanced growth paths. We define Thermo-GDP
(a damage-adjusted output measure) and connect it to existing green accounting
frameworks (SEEA, Adjusted Net Savings, Inclusive Wealth). Extensions to Ramsey
optimal growth, Hotelling resource extraction, and DSGE environmental shocks are
previewed for companion papers.
Keywords: economic growth, ecological economics, thermodynamics, health capital,
environmental macroeconomics, productivity

1. Introduction
Modern economies convert low-entropy resources into high-entropy products, services,
and waste at unprecedented scale. The Second Law of Thermodynamics guarantees that
every production process generates some waste heat and disorder. This does not impose
a hard ceiling on growth, efficiency can improve enormously before approaching
theoretical limits, and knowledge-intensive and service-based production have very
different throughput profiles than heavy industry. What it does impose is an irreducible
physical cost that varies with technology, energy mix, and economic structure, and that
standard macroeconomic models systematically under-account for. [1] Canonical growth
models from Solow (1956) to modern DSGE treat the economy as a closed circular flow
of income, with environmental impacts relegated to externality corrections applied after
the fact.
This omission has become untenable. The OECD projects that outdoor air pollution
alone could reduce global GDP by approximately 1% by 2060 through labor productivity
losses and healthcare expenditures. [2] The IPCC AR6 warns that unmitigated warming
could reduce global economic output by 10–23% by 2100. [3] Empirical work in
thermoeconomics has demonstrated that useful exergy explains a far larger share of
historical growth than conventional capital–labor models suggest, substantially
narrowing the Solow residual. [4,5]
The goal of this paper is to augment the Solow growth model with state variables
that make the costs of thermodynamic waste, health degradation, and resource
depletion visible within the standard framework. The augmentation is designed to be
conservative: when environmental costs are zero, the model collapses exactly to the
textbook Solow result. When those costs are positive, the model generates a lower
steady-state growth path, providing a formal basis for the intuition that dirty growth
today cannibalizes future output.
We focus on the Solow model because it is the canonical framework through which
economists think about long-run growth, and because its simplicity makes the
augmentation transparent. Extensions to the Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans optimal growth
framework (where the discount rate becomes endogenous to environmental conditions),
the Hotelling exhaustible resource model (where the optimal extraction path accounts
for thermodynamic damage costs), and the New Keynesian DSGE framework (where
environmental degradation generates a new class of supply shocks) are previewed in
Section 7 and developed in companion papers.

1.1 What This Framework Does Not Claim
To prevent misreading, we state explicitly what this paper does not argue:
• It does not imply hard thermodynamic limits to near-term economic growth.
Efficiency improvements, compositional shifts, and innovation can reduce TO
intensity substantially.
• It does not claim that pollution is the dominant driver of the productivity
slowdown. We propose it as a testable contributing mechanism, not a
monocausal explanation.
• It does not replace standard macroeconomics. It augments the Solow model with
additional state variables; when those variables are at baseline, the standard
model obtains exactly.
• It does not reject technological progress or decoupling. Clean growth (rising
output with falling TO intensity) is the policy objective the framework identifies.
• It does not claim GDP is useless. It proposes Thermo-GDP as a complementary
dashboard metric, not a replacement.
1.2 Relation to Existing Literature
This framework builds on Brock and Taylor’s Green Solow Model, [15] Nordhaus’s DICE
integrated assessment model, [18] and the broader IAM tradition. It differs from these
predecessors in four specific ways. First, health capital enters as an endogenous state
variable affecting both labor quality and TFP simultaneously, creating a double feedback
channel absent from standard IAMs. Second, thermodynamic waste is modeled as a
production-side throughput variable (τ = TO/Y) rather than purely as downstream
damage. The sustainability multiplier ϕ(τ) operates on the production function directly,
not as a post-hoc damage subtraction. Third, the Ω amplification mechanism—
environmental degradation reducing steady-state output by more than the direct
penalty because it also depresses equilibrium capital accumulation—is a structural
result not present in Green Solow or DICE. Fourth, Thermo-GDP is internally linked to
the same shadow-price structure as the production model, ensuring consistency
between the growth framework and the accounting framework. The contribution is not
that any single element is entirely new, but that their integration into a single
analytically tractable model produces emergent results (the amplification effect, the
growth trap) that do not appear in the separate literatures.

2. Theoretical Architecture
2.1 The Economy as a Dissipative Structure
Following Georgescu-Roegen (1971), we conceptualize the economy as an open
dissipative system that maintains internal complexity by importing low-entropy
resources and exporting high-entropy waste. [1] This implies that production is
throughput (requiring continuous material and energy flows), waste generation is
endogenous (a physical law, not a market failure), and sustainability requires that the
biosphere’s finite sink capacity not be exceeded. [6]
2.2 The Production Function
We adopt a four-factor production function that nests the standard Cobb–Douglas as a
special case. Following Ayres and Warr (2005, 2009), who showed that including useful
exergy as a factor of production substantially resolves the Solow residual, [4,5] and Santos
et al. (2018), who found exergy output elasticities of 0.29–0.46 across multiple
countries, [7] we write:
Yₜ = Aₜ · Kₜᵅ · Lₜᵝ · Uₜᵘ where α + β + u = 1 (1)
where Kₜ is physical capital, Lₜ is labor, Uₜ is useful exergy (primary exergy multiplied by
conversion efficiency fₜ), and Aₜ is residual TFP. In the core model (Section 3), we work
with the standard two-factor reduction Y = AKᵅ(AL)¹⁻ᵅ for analytical tractability,
treating exergy as embedded in TFP. The four-factor version is used for simulation and
empirical calibration.
2.3 Physical Thermodynamic Output
We define physical thermodynamic output as the aggregate material and energy waste
generated by production. Crucially, TO is measured in physical units (tonnes, joules,
cubic meters)—it is a vector of waste flows, not a damage measure:
TOₜ = (Eₜ, Pₜ, Mₜ) (2)
where:
• Eₜ = energy waste: lost heat, flaring, transmission losses (joules)
• Pₜ = pollution emissions: CO₂e, PM₂.₅, NOₓ, SO₂, heavy metals, wastewater
(tonnes)
• Mₜ = material waste: landfill volumes, tailings, single-use plastics (tonnes)
Note what is not in TO: health damage, ecosystem damage, and welfare losses. These are
downstream consequences of physical TO, not components of it. This separation is
essential to avoid double-counting. The causal chain runs:
Page 4
Production → Physical TO (waste flows) → Shadow-priced damage → Health effects →
Feedback to production
We define TO intensity as a dimensionless scalar index of waste-per-unit-output:
τₜ ≡ ‖TOₜ‖ / (p₀ · Yₜ) (3)
where ‖TOₜ‖ is a weighted norm that aggregates across waste types using damage-
equivalence factors (e.g., global warming potentials convert all greenhouse gases to
CO₂e tonnes), and p₀ is a fixed normalization constant with units of [currency per
tonne-equivalent] chosen so that τ is dimensionless and bounded in [0, 1]. This is the
same dimensional bridge that applied climate economists already use when expressing
damages as “% of GDP”: physical flows are implicitly converted to a common monetary
scale via a reference price. We make this normalization explicit. TO intensity is a
production-side measure of how dirty the economy’s technology is. It is distinct from,
and not inflated by, cleanup spending.
2.4 Damage Valuation
Physical TO causes economic damage through environmental degradation and health
effects. We value this damage using shadow prices derived from the established social-
cost literature:
Dₜ = λ′ · TOₜ = λₑ Eₜ + λ p Pₜ + λₘ Mₜ (4)
where λₙ is the marginal social damage per unit of waste type n. For greenhouse gases,
the EPA’s 2023 central estimate is ~$190/tCO₂ at a 2% discount rate, [9] with Rennert et
al. (2022) arriving at ~$185/tCO₂. [10] Analogous values exist for methane
(~$1,600/tCH₄), nitrous oxide (~$5,400/tN₂O), and PM₂.₅ (valued through mortality
and morbidity costs). [9]
The total damage Dₜ has units of dollars per period. It represents the present value of all
downstream harm: climate damage, ecosystem degradation, and health effects caused
by one period’s physical waste flows. Health damage is a subset of Dₜ, not an additive
component alongside it. This layered structure (physical flows, then prices, then
downstream channels) eliminates the double-counting risk that arises when health
damage appears both as a TO component and as a separate variable.
2.5 Health Capital
Health capital Hₜ is a state variable representing the productive capacity of the
population’s health. It is driven down by pollution exposure (a downstream
consequence of physical TO) and driven up by preventive health investment and
baseline medical progress. The empirical foundations are strong: Graff Zivin and Neidell
Page 5
(2012) found that ozone exposure significantly reduced agricultural worker productivity,
[11] while European firm-level studies estimate that a 1 μg/m³ PM₂.₅ increase reduces
labor productivity by approximately 0.55%. [12]
Per-Capita Exposure
We define an exposure index that translates aggregate pollution into a per-person dose
proxy:
Xₜ = κ · Pₜ / Popₜ (5)
where Pₜ is the pollution component of physical TO (Equation 2), κ is a dose–response
scaling parameter calibrated to epidemiological data, and Popₜ is population. Note that
Xₜ depends on physical pollution flows, not on monetized damage—maintaining the
clean causal chain.
Health Stock Dynamics
The law of motion for health capital is:
Ḣₜ = η₀ + η₁ hₜ − δₕ Hₜ − χ Xₜ (6)
where:
• η₀ = baseline health improvement from medical/sanitation progress
• hₜ = HEₜᵖʳᵉᵛ / Yₜ = preventive health spending as a share of output
• δₕ = natural health depreciation (aging, chronic disease)
• χ Xₜ = health damage from environmental exposure, χ > 0
In steady state, H* = (η₀ + η₁ h* − χ X*) / δₕ. Higher pollution exposure (X) reduces
steady-state health capital; higher preventive spending (h) raises it.
Health-Adjusted Inputs
Health capital enters the production function through two channels:
Lₜᵉᶠᶠ = Lₜ · θ(Hₜ) and Aₜᵉᶠᶠ = Aₜ · ζ(Hₜ) (7)
where θ(H) = (H/H̄)b captures labor quality (absenteeism, physical capacity, work
hours) and ζ(H) = (H/H̄)ᵐ captures the TFP channel (cognition, error rates, learning
capacity), with b, m > 0 and H̄ = baseline health. When H = H̄, both functions equal
unity and the standard production function obtains. This creates the feedback loop at
the heart of the model: production → pollution → exposure → health decline → reduced
effective inputs → lower output.
Page 6
The TFP channel ζ(H) is worth flagging: if pollution has been eroding cognitive
performance in advanced economies, then measured TFP could be declining even as
technological knowledge accumulates. This offers a testable contributing mechanism
(not a complete explanation) for the observed productivity slowdown. A companion
paper develops this hypothesis with firm-level data.
2.6 Sustainability Metrics
Following the critique that a single index can conflate production efficiency with
remediation effort, we define two separate sustainability indicators:
TO Intensity (production-side cleanliness):
τₜ = ‖TOₜ‖ / (p₀ · Yₜ) (3, restated)
This measures how much physical waste is generated per unit of output. It depends on
technology, energy mix, and process efficiency. It is not affected by cleanup spending.
Lower is better.
Net Ecological Position (balance-sheet sustainability):
NEPₜ = G(Rₜ) + Cₜʳᵉˢᵗᵒʳᵉ − Dₜ (8)
where G(Rₜ) is natural regeneration, Cₜʳᵉˢᵗᵒʳᵉ is restoration/abatement investment, and
Dₜ is monetized damage (Equation 4). NEP > 0 means the economy is building natural
capital on net; NEP < 0 means it is drawing down. This is analogous to the World Bank’s
Adjusted Net Savings but grounded in our damage-pricing framework. [21]
The Sustainability Multiplier. For the core Solow model, we define the
sustainability multiplier as a function of TO intensity:
ϕ(τₜ) = (1 − τₜ)ψ where ψ > 0 (9)
Axiomatic justification. We require ϕ to satisfy four properties: (A1) ϕ(0) = 1—zero
waste imposes no penalty; (A2) ϕ is strictly decreasing in τ—more waste means less
effective output; (A3) ϕ is convex—marginal damage is increasing; and (A4) as τ
approaches its upper normalization bound, effective productive efficiency approaches
zero. The family (1−τ)ψ is the simplest function satisfying all four axioms. The
parameter ψ controls the convexity of the penalty: ψ < 1 yields a gentle curve (small
increases in waste have modest effects), ψ = 1 is linear, and ψ > 1 generates a steep
penalty for dirty economies.
We emphasize that the qualitative results of the model, the amplified steady-state
reduction and the signs of all comparative statics, hold for any ϕ that is decreasing,
convex, and satisfies ϕ(0) = 1. The specific power form is chosen for tractability. The
Page 7
parameter ψ is, in principle, empirically estimable using cross-country regressions of
TFP on TO intensity; a calibration strategy is outlined as a direction for future work.
Note that ϕ depends only on TO intensity, not on restoration spending, resolving the
perverse-incentive problem identified in the earlier formulation.
2.7 Natural Capital Dynamics
The stock of a renewable resource Rₜ evolves as:
dRₜ/dt = G(Rₜ) − Cₜ (10)
where G(R) = rR(1 − R/K) is logistic regeneration with carrying capacity K and intrinsic
growth rate r, and Cₜ is extraction. Sustainable extraction requires Cₜ ≤ G(Rₜ) in the long
run. For non-renewable resources (G ≈ 0), the Hotelling rule governs the optimal
extraction path. [14] Our framework modifies the Hotelling condition by adding the
marginal thermodynamic damage cost of extraction to the user cost. However, the
correct sign and functional form of this modification require a careful Hamiltonian
derivation that is beyond the scope of the present paper. Figure 1 summarizes the
model architecture and the causal pathways linking production, thermodynamic output,
health capital, and long-run growth.
3. The Core Model: Thermo-Ecological Solow
We now assemble the components into a single, formally specified growth model.
3.1 Setup
We work with the standard Solow assumptions: exogenous savings rate s, capital
depreciation rate δ, labor force growth rate n, and Harrod-neutral technical progress at
rate g. The production function is modified by the thermo-ecological multiplier:
Yₜ = Ωₜ · F(Kₜ, Aₜ Lₜ) = Ωₜ · Kₜᵅ (Aₜ Lₜ)¹⁻ᵅ (11)
where Ωₜ ≡ ϕ(τₜ) · ζ(Hₜ) · [θ(Hₜ)]¹⁻ᵅ is the thermo-ecological multiplier combining the
sustainability penalty and both health channels. Note:
• ϕ(τₜ) = (1 − τₜ)ψ — sustainability multiplier (Eq. 9)
• ζ(Hₜ) = (Hₜ / H̄)ᵐ — health–TFP channel
• θ(Hₜ) = (Hₜ / H̄)b — health–labor quality channel (enters with exponent 1−α
from the production function)
Capital accumulates in the standard way:
K̇ₜ = sYₜ − δKₜ (12)

3.2 Intensive Form
Define capital per effective worker: kₜ ≡ Kₜ / (Aₜ Lₜ). Output per effective worker is:
yₜ = Ωₜ · kₜᵅ (13)
The fundamental Solow equation in intensive form becomes:
k̇ₜ = s · Ωₜ · kₜᵅ − (n + g + δ) · kₜ (14)
This is identical to the standard Solow equation except for the Ωₜ term multiplying the
production function. When Ω = 1 (no environmental costs), Equation (14) reduces to the
textbook version.
3.3 Steady State
At the steady state k̇ = 0:
s · Ω* · (k*)ᵅ = (n + g + δ) · k* (15)
Solving for k*:
k* = [s · Ω* / (n + g + δ)]^(1/(1−α)) (16)
Steady-state output per effective worker:
y* = Ω* · [s · Ω* / (n + g + δ)]^(α/(1−α)) (17)
which simplifies to:
y* = (Ω*)^(1/(1−α)) · [s / (n + g + δ)]^(α/(1−α)) (18)
This is the central result. Equation (18) is the standard Solow steady-state output
multiplied by (Ω*)^(1/(1−α)). Since 0 < Ω ≤ 1 and 1/(1−α) > 1, the thermo-ecological
multiplier has an amplified effect on steady-state output: a 10% reduction in Ω reduces
y* by more than 10% because it also depresses equilibrium capital accumulation.
3.4 Decomposing the Multiplier
Expanding Ω*:
Ω* = (1 − τ*)ψ · (H*/H̄)ᵐ · (H*/H̄)^(b(1−α)) (19)
This reveals three distinct drags on the steady state:
• Waste drag: (1 − τ*)ψ. Higher TO intensity (dirtier technology) lowers potential
output.
• Cognitive drag: (H*/H̄)ᵐ. Pollution-induced health deterioration impairs TFP
through reduced cognition, increased errors, slower learning.
Page 10
• Labor drag: (H*/H̄)^(b(1−α)). Health deterioration reduces effective labor
supply through absenteeism, shorter work hours, reduced physical capacity.
In the standard Solow model (H = H̄, τ = 0), all three terms equal unity, Ω = 1, and the
standard steady state obtains. This confirms that the augmented model nests the
original as a special case.
3.5 Comparative Statics
Differentiating Equation (16) with respect to the key environmental parameters:
∂k*/∂τ < 0 — dirtier production → lower capital intensity (20a)
∂k*/∂H > 0 — healthier population → higher capital intensity (20b)
∂k*/∂ψ < 0 (for τ > 0) — steeper penalty → lower steady state (20c)
These signs are unambiguous and match economic intuition. A complete derivation is
provided in Appendix B.
3.6 The Thermodynamic Growth Trap
The growth trap arises when Ωₜ is declining over time; i.e., when TO intensity is rising
and/or health capital is falling. In this case, the steady state itself is moving downward:
the economy is converging toward a target that is retreating. Short-run GDP growth
remains positive (the economy is still accumulating capital), but the long-run balanced
growth path is lower than it would be under stable environmental conditions.
Critically, the trap is invisible in conventional GDP. An economy in the trap shows
positive output growth, normal unemployment, and no obvious distress while its
sustainable productive capacity is silently eroding. Only a damage-adjusted measure like
Thermo-GDP (Section 4) reveals the deterioration. This is perhaps the framework’s
most policy-relevant finding.
This mechanism generates a testable contributing hypothesis for secular stagnation:
if TO intensity and health damage have been rising in advanced economies since the
1970s (a period coinciding with the observed productivity slowdown), the
thermodynamic drag term Ωₜ may account for part of the deceleration. It also offers a
candidate explanation for convergence failure: developing economies with high τ,
low H, and depleted R face effective steady states far below the standard Solow
prediction, potentially constituting poverty traps. Both hypotheses are developed and
tested in companion papers.
Clean growth and endogenous τ decline. It is essential to emphasize that the
framework is not anti-growth and does not predict that τ must rise. Historical evidence
shows that TO intensity has fallen substantially in many advanced economies through
Page 11
energy efficiency improvements, compositional shifts toward services, regulatory
pressure, and technological innovation. Between 1990 and 2020, the EU reduced
greenhouse gas emissions per unit of GDP by roughly 50%. The model’s prediction is
conditional: when τ rises (or fails to fall fast enough relative to growing output), the
consequences propagate through Ω to the steady state. Clean growth—rising Y with
falling τ—is not only possible within the model but is precisely the policy objective it
identifies. An economy that drives τ toward zero converges to the standard Solow steady
state from below, with Ω → 1. The thermodynamic framing does not imply secular
pessimism; it identifies the conditions under which optimism is warranted.
4. Thermo-GDP
We define Thermo-GDP as:
Thermo-GDPₜ = GDPₜ − Dₜ = GDPₜ − λ′ · TOₜ (21)
where Dₜ is the monetized damage from Equation (4). This is a Hicksian “green net
product”: the maximum output the economy could sustain indefinitely without
depleting natural and health capital stocks. It builds on and synthesizes:
• UN SEEA-EA: the international statistical standard for ecosystem accounting.
SEEA provides the data architecture; our model provides the theoretical welfare
interpretation. [19]
• Adjusted Net Savings: the World Bank’s measure of “genuine savings.”
Thermo-GDP extends this by incorporating health capital. [21]
• Inclusive Wealth: the UNEP stock-based sustainability indicator. Our resource
dynamics and health stock provide the flow counterparts to inclusive wealth
stocks. [22]
• GPI / ISEW: earlier cost-subtraction approaches. Our framework provides
microfounded shadow prices via the social-cost literature rather than ad hoc
adjustments. [20]
The key advantage of Thermo-GDP over these predecessors is theoretical grounding:
the damage subtraction Dₜ is derived from the same shadow prices that enter the
modified production function, ensuring internal consistency between the growth model
and the accounting framework.
5. Planetary Boundary Constraints
Rockström et al. (2009) identified nine Earth-system thresholds defining a safe
operating space. [23] Richardson et al. (2023) report that six of nine have been
Page 12
transgressed. [24,25] These can enter the model as penalty functions in the welfare
objective:
Φ(Rₜ) = Σₙ ωₙ · max(0, R̄ₙ − Rₙ,ₜ)² (22)
where ωₙ weights boundary n and (R̄ₙ − Rₙ,ₜ) measures transgression. This is consistent
with Raworth’s “Doughnut Economics” framing [26] but provides a pathway toward
formal optimization within a Ramsey-style growth framework.
6. Empirical Strategy and Testability
The framework generates several testable predictions:
• Prediction 1: Countries with lower TO intensity (τ = TO/GDP) should exhibit
higher future TFP growth, controlling for standard determinants.
• Prediction 2: Sector-level PM₂.₅ exposure should negatively predict labor
productivity, with stronger effects in cognitively intensive industries. [12]
• Prediction 3: Thermo-GDP should diverge from conventional GDP most
sharply in resource-intensive economies, while converging in economies
undergoing green transitions.
• Prediction 4: Pollution reduction should generate positive growth effects
exceeding the narrowly measured health cost savings (i.e., the feedback loop
amplifies returns to abatement). [11,13]
• Prediction 5: Cross-country income convergence rates should be moderated by
initial TO intensity and health capital, with high-τ, low-H countries converging
more slowly.
Data sources include: SEEA accounts (27 EU member states), WHO Global Health
Estimates, satellite-derived PM₂.₅ data (van Donkelaar et al.), IEA energy balances, and
the World Bank’s Adjusted Net Savings database. These datasets provide a feasible
foundation for future cross-country calibration and simulation exercises. [19,21]
6.1 Anchor Regression: The Health–Productivity Channel
The empirically strongest entry point for testing the framework is the health–
productivity feedback loop, where causal estimates and measurable elasticities already
exist in the literature. We propose the following baseline panel specification, using
standard econometric notation where symbols are local to this regression and distinct
from model parameters elsewhere in the paper:
Δ ln(TFP)ᵢₜ = β₁ τᵢ,ₜ₋₁ + β₂ ln(Hᵢ,ₜ₋₁) + γ′ Xᵢₜ + αᵢ + δₜ + εᵢₜ
Page 13
where i indexes countries, t indexes years, τᵢ,ₜ₋₁ is lagged TO intensity (proxied by CO₂e
emissions per unit of GDP from SEEA/IEA), Hᵢ,ₜ₋₁ is lagged health capital (proxied by
HALEs from WHO, or inverse PM₂.₅ exposure from satellite data), Xᵢₜ is a vector of
standard controls (human capital, trade openness, institutional quality), αᵢ are country
fixed effects, and δₜ are year fixed effects. The model predicts β₁ < 0 (higher waste
intensity depresses future TFP) and β₂ > 0 (higher health capital boosts future TFP).
Satellite-derived PM₂.₅ exposure serves as an instrument for the health channel,
exploiting atmospheric transport patterns for identification. This specification can be
estimated with existing data; it does not require waiting for new measurement
infrastructure.
7. Theoretical Extensions of the Core Framework
The Solow model developed above is deliberately the simplest possible vehicle for the
core ideas. Richer frameworks require the following extensions:
7.1 Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans
Replace the exogenous savings rate with an optimizing representative agent who
chooses consumption, savings, and restoration investment. The modified Euler equation
is:
Ċ/C = (1/σ)[f′(k) · Ω − δ − ρ + μ(∂Ψ/∂NEP)(∂NEP/∂Cʳᵉˢᵗ)] (23)
where the last term captures the welfare gain from restoration investment. This
extension makes the effective social discount rate endogenous: when environmental
damage is high and restoration is productive, the model implies a lower rate than the
standard Ramsey calibration, closer to Stern than Nordhaus, but derived from model
structure rather than ethical assumption.
7.2 Hotelling Resource Extraction
The optimal extraction path for exhaustible resources is modified by the marginal
thermodynamic damage cost. The social planner’s Hamiltonian includes the shadow
price of natural capital and the damage from extraction-related TO. The resulting price
path differs from the standard Hotelling rule; the direction and magnitude of the
deviation depend on the relative rates of damage accumulation and discount. A full
Hamiltonian derivation, including the correct treatment of signs and dynamic shadow
prices, is left for future work.
7.3 DSGE Environmental Shocks
TO-driven environmental shocks such as droughts, heat waves, and resource price
spikes, can be incorporated as negative TFP shocks (via ϕ and ζ), labor supply shocks
Page 14
(via θ), and cost-push shocks (via resource scarcity) in standard DSGE estimation
toolkits. This generates a formal channel through which environmental degradation
contributes to stagflation.
7.4 Optimal Carbon Price Path
The shadow prices λ in Equation (4) are equilibrium objects whose optimal time paths
depend on the full model structure. Rather than a static social cost of carbon, the
framework implies a dynamic price trajectory. Deriving and calibrating this trajectory is
left for future work.
8. Policy Implications
8.1 Pigouvian Pricing of TO
The shadow prices λ provide the theoretically correct tax rates on each waste
component. For carbon: ~$190/tCO₂. [9] For PM₂.₅: the labor productivity co-benefits
alone may be worth $950–$3,000 per household per year per μg/m³ reduction. [12]
8.2 Sustainability-Weighted National Accounting
Adopt Thermo-GDP as a complementary dashboard metric alongside conventional
GDP, consistent with the Stiglitz–Sen–Fitoussi Commission recommendations. [27]
8.3 Directed Innovation Policy
Subsidize technologies that reduce τ (TO intensity). Following Acemoglu et al. (2012), [16]
early subsidies for clean technologies can permanently redirect the innovation path.
8.4 Health-Inclusive Cost–Benefit Analysis
Regulatory cost–benefit analyses should incorporate the productivity co-benefits of
pollution reduction through the health capital channel. Current practice accounts for
avoided mortality but typically omits the morbidity–productivity–TFP feedback loop
formalized here.

10. Limitations
We acknowledge several limitations of the present framework that circumscribe the
claims we make and define the work remaining for the companion papers.
First, the thermo-ecological multiplier Ω is reduced-form. The sustainability
multiplier ϕ(τ) = (1−τ)ψ is axiomatically motivated and analytically convenient, but it is
not micro-founded from optimizing firm or consumer behavior. The qualitative results
are robust to alternative functional forms (any decreasing, convex ϕ with ϕ(0) = 1
preserves the signs of all comparative statics and the amplification result), but the
quantitative predictions depend on ψ, which must be estimated empirically. Until such
calibrations are performed, the framework should be interpreted primarily as a
qualitative and structural model rather than a quantitative forecasting engine.
Second, TO intensity dynamics are not fully endogenized in the present paper.
We treat τ as a slow-moving state variable influenced by technology, policy, and
economic structure, but we do not derive its evolution from optimizing behavior. In
reality, τ responds endogenously to prices, regulation, and innovation incentives.
Extensions incorporating directed technical change and Ramsey-style intertemporal
optimization partially address this issue, but a fully endogenous τ pathway remains an
open research problem.
Third, the aggregation of heterogeneous waste streams into a scalar TO intensity
index is a significant simplification. Energy waste, greenhouse gases, particulate matter,
and material waste have different spatial distributions, temporal profiles, and damage
pathways. The weighted-norm aggregation in Equation (3) is a tractable first
approximation, but it suppresses heterogeneity that may matter for policy design.
Sectoral disaggregation and multi-region calibration represent important directions for
future empirical work.
Fourth, causal identification in the health–productivity channel is challenging.
While the micro-econometric literature establishes causal effects of pollution on worker
productivity in specific settings, [11,12] extrapolating these to macro-level TFP effects
Page 16
involves aggregation assumptions that have not been fully tested. The regression
specification in Section 6.1 provides a starting point, but instrument validity and general
equilibrium feedbacks require careful treatment.
Fifth, the model currently abstracts from distributional effects. Thermodynamic
waste, health damage, and resource depletion fall disproportionately on lower-income
populations and regions. The representative-agent Solow framework cannot capture
these distributional dynamics. Heterogeneous-agent extensions would be valuable but
are beyond the scope of this paper.
11. Conclusion
This paper augments the Solow growth model with three coupled state variables,
including physical thermodynamic output, health capital, and natural capital, to model
the economy as an open dissipative system constrained by the biosphere. The central
result is the thermo-ecological multiplier Ω, which enters the steady state with amplified
effect: a 10% degradation in environmental and health conditions reduces long-run
output per worker by more than 10%, because it also depresses equilibrium capital
accumulation.
The framework is designed to be analytically transparent. It nests the standard Solow
model as a special case (Ω = 1). Its predictions are testable with existing data: TO
intensity predicts TFP growth, health damage feeds back to output, and dirty economies
converge to lower growth paths. And its primary applied innovation, Thermo-GDP,
connects directly to established accounting systems (SEEA, Adjusted Net Savings,
Inclusive Wealth) while providing microfounded shadow prices.
The contribution is deliberately modest in scope: one canonical growth model, cleanly
specified, with formal derivations. The questions this framework opens, including the
optimal carbon price path, the endogenous discount rate, and the role of
thermodynamic drag in secular stagnation and convergence failure, represent promising
directions for future theoretical and empirical research.

Appendix B: Formal Derivations
B.1 Steady-State Derivation
Step 1. Begin with the fundamental Solow equation in per-effective-worker form:
k̇ = s · Ω · kᵅ − (n + g + δ) · k
Step 2. At the steady state k̇ = 0, so:
s · Ω* · (k*)ᵅ = (n + g + δ) · k*
Step 3. Divide both sides by k*:
s · Ω* · (k*)ᵅ⁻¹ = n + g + δ
Step 4. Solve for k*:
(k*)ᵅ⁻¹ = (n + g + δ) / (s · Ω*)
k* = [(n + g + δ) / (s · Ω*)]^(1/(α−1))
k* = [s · Ω* / (n + g + δ)]^(1/(1−α)) … (16)
Step 5. Steady-state output per effective worker:
y* = Ω* · (k*)ᵅ = Ω* · [s · Ω* / (n + g + δ)]^(α/(1−α))
Collecting powers of Ω*:
y* = (Ω*)^(1 + α/(1−α)) · [s / (n + g + δ)]^(α/(1−α))
y* = (Ω*)^(1/(1−α)) · [s / (n + g + δ)]^(α/(1−α)) … (18)
Interpretation: The standard Solow steady-state output is [s/(n+g+δ)]^(α/(1−α)).
Our augmented model multiplies this by (Ω*)^(1/(1−α)). Since 0 < Ω ≤ 1 and 1/(1−α)
> 1 (for α ∈ (0,1)), the environmental penalty is amplified: if α = 0.3, a 10% reduction
in Ω lowers y* by approximately 14.0% (= 1 − 0.9^(1/0.7)).
B.2 Comparative Statics
Proposition 1. ∂k*/∂Ω > 0.
From Equation (16): k* = [sΩ*/(n+g+δ)]^(1/(1−α)). Since 1/(1−α) > 0 and s,
(n+g+δ) > 0, k* is strictly increasing in Ω*. □
Proposition 2. ∂k*/∂τ < 0.
By the chain rule: ∂k*/∂τ = (∂k*/∂Ω)(∂Ω/∂ϕ)(∂ϕ/∂τ). We have ∂k*/∂Ω > 0 (Prop 1),
∂Ω/∂ϕ > 0 (since Ω = ϕ·ζ·θ¹⁻ᵅ and ζ, θ > 0), and ∂ϕ/∂τ = −ψ(1−τ)^(ψ−1) < 0. Hence
∂k*/∂τ < 0. □
Proposition 3. ∂k*/∂H > 0.
Page 19
By the chain rule: ∂k*/∂H = (∂k*/∂Ω)[ϕ · ∂(ζ·θ¹⁻ᵅ)/∂H]. Since ζ = (H/H̄)ᵐ and θ¹⁻ᵅ
= (H/H̄)^(b(1−α)), we get ζ·θ¹⁻ᵅ = (H/H̄)^(m+b(1−α)), which is strictly increasing in
H. □
Proposition 4 (Amplification). The elasticity of y* with respect to Ω exceeds
unity.
From (18): ∂ln(y*)/∂ln(Ω) = 1/(1−α) > 1 for all α ∈ (0,1). With α = 0.3, the elasticity
is 1.43. With α = 0.4, it is 1.67. This means that environmental degradation has a
leveraged effect on long-run output, because it depresses capital accumulation as
well as direct production efficiency. □
B.3 Transition Dynamics
The speed of convergence to steady state is determined by linearizing Equation (14)
around k*:
d(ln k)/dt ≈ −(1−α)(n + g + δ) · [ln(k) − ln(k*)]
The convergence rate λᶜ = (1−α)(n+g+δ) is identical to the standard Solow
convergence rate. The thermo-ecological augmentation changes where the economy
converges to (k* is lower when Ω < 1) but not how fast it converges. This means the
growth trap is not about slow convergence, it is about converging to the wrong place.
B.4 Health Capital Steady State
Setting Ḣ = 0 in Equation (6):
0 = η₀ + η₁ h* − δₕ H* − χ X*
H* = (η₀ + η₁ h* − χ X*) / δₕ
Substituting X* = κ P*/Pop* and noting that P* depends on Y* and τ*:
H* = [η₀ + η₁ h* − χκτ* Y* / Pop*] / δₕ
Since Y* itself depends on H* (through Ω), this defines H* implicitly. The system has
a fixed-point structure: H* and Y* must be solved simultaneously. For existence and
uniqueness, we require that the feedback is not too strong: χκτ must be small enough
that the health equation’s ‘damage slope’ does not exceed its ‘recovery slope.’ Under
empirically plausible parameter values, this condition is satisfied and the fixed point
is unique and stable.
